keyTakeaways:
  - The 2026 snapshot puts the median existing-home price at $398,000 against a 6.23% 30-year fixed rate.
  - Buying and renting trade off on different dimensions — time horizon, stability, wealth building, and upfront cash — not just monthly payment.
  - The 28%-of-income housing payment target and 36% total-debt target are ceilings, and cash to close and emergency reserves matter as much as the payment itself.
  - Going from 3.5% down to 20% down on the median home lowers the P&I payment from about $2,360 to about $1,956 per month, but requires a larger loan-to-value trade-off in cash at closing.

actionSteps:
  - Compute 28% of your own gross monthly income and compare it against a target home's estimated payment.
  - Add your other debt payments to the housing payment and check the total against the 36% debt target.
  - Confirm you would still have 6–12 months of expenses in reserve after paying closing costs.
  - If evaluating a rental as an investment, run the 1% rule and a 5% vacancy assumption before treating the numbers as a guarantee.

The median-home headline number gets all the attention, but this map treats
it as one input among several. The 2026 snapshot sets the baseline — median
price, 30- and 15-year rates, typical closing costs, and the reserve cushion
worth having after closing — before any buy-vs-rent decision gets made.

The Buy vs Rent Scorecard resists a single winner. Buying builds equity and
locks in a payment; renting keeps cash flexible and shifts maintenance risk
elsewhere. The Watch-Outs column is the map's way of saying neither side is
free of tradeoffs.

The payment examples make the down-payment tradeoff concrete: moving from
3.5% down to 20% down on the same median home lowers the monthly P&I payment,
but every example is principal and interest only — the Cash-Flow Lens section
is the reminder that taxes, insurance, PMI, and maintenance still sit on top.
