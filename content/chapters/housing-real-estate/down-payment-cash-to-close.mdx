keyTakeaways:
  - At the $412,400 median home price, the down payment ranges from $14,434 (3.5%) to $82,480 (20%), but cash to close is always higher than the down payment alone.
  - Estimated cash to close spans $22.7k–$35.1k at 3.5% down up to $90.7k–$103.1k at 20% down.
  - Closing costs alone (about 2%–5%) add $8,248–$20,620 on top of whatever down payment is chosen.
  - The 20% + Reserve option is framed as the "best comfort zone" specifically because it keeps extra post-close cash for repairs and surprises.

actionSteps:
  - Calculate your own estimated cash to close at 3.5%, 10%, and 20% down using the map's ranges as a reference point.
  - Budget for closing costs (about 2%–5% of price) and the roughly 1%/year maintenance rule separately from the down payment.
  - Set aside earnest money (often 1%–3%) before you make an offer, not after.
  - Check whether you'd have a post-closing reserve left over after your down payment and closing costs — if not, consider a smaller down payment plus a reserve.

Buyers tend to fixate on the down payment percentage and forget everything
else that has to be paid at the closing table. This map anchors the whole
picture to one scenario — a $412,400 median home — and walks the down
payment from 3.5% up through 20%, showing cash to close at every step.

The gap between the down payment alone and the total estimated cash to close
is the map's central point. At 3.5% down, $14,434 turns into $22.7k–$35.1k
once closing costs, earnest money, and other fees are added. At 20% down, an
$82,480 down payment becomes $90.7k–$103.1k out the door.

The "Other Upfront Costs" table and the fourth ladder rung — 20% + Reserve —
both push the same idea further: buying costs don't stop at closing.
Inspection, moving, initial repairs, and an emergency housing reserve are all
additional, and the map frames a fully funded reserve as more valuable than
squeezing every dollar into the down payment.
