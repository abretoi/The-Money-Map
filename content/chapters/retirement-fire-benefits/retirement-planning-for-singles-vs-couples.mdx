keyTakeaways:
  - A couple both maxing 401(k) and IRA accounts can save $61,000/year tax-advantaged, versus $30,500/year for a single saver — more than double.
  - Couples gain spousal and survivor Social Security options, coordinated claiming, and joint tax flexibility that singles cannot access.
  - Singles face higher per-person fixed costs and carry more longevity risk since there is no second income source to fall back on.
  - Survivor benefits can replace up to 100% of a deceased spouse's benefit, a significant retirement-income cushion unique to couples.

actionSteps:
  - Calculate your own household's annual tax-advantaged savings capacity using the four benchmark figures on the map.

  - If a couple, confirm your retirement ages and Social Security claiming strategy are actually coordinated, not just assumed.
  - Review your beneficiary designations and estate documents against the Best Practices checklist.

Retirement math does not scale evenly with household size. The map's savings
capacity figures make that explicit: a single saver maxing a 401(k) and IRA
can put away $30,500 a year, but a couple doing the same thing can save
$61,000 — more than double, not because each person saves more, but because
there are two people saving at all.

The Single vs. Couple Retirement Map works through eight dimensions —
savings, fixed costs, taxes, healthcare, Social Security, withdrawal
flexibility, estate planning, and longevity risk — and a consistent pattern
emerges. Coupled households get more levers: shared expenses, coordinated
claiming, spousal benefits, joint filing. Singles get simplicity, but less
built-in resilience if something goes wrong.

The Planning Watchouts section is the map's acknowledgment that neither
situation is risk-free. Singles need to protect harder against income shocks
with no second earner to fall back on; couples need to actively coordinate
decisions that would otherwise happen automatically for an individual.
