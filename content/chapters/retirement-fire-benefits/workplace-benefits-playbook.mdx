keyTakeaways:
  - The benefit stack has a best-use order, and it starts with capturing the full employer match before anything else.
  - An HSA carries a triple tax advantage and is worth funding right after the match, if you're eligible.
  - FSAs and Dependent Care FSAs only make sense for spending you already expect.
  - RSUs and bonuses need active management to avoid overconcentration and tax surprises.

actionSteps:
  - Confirm your employer's match formula and check whether you are capturing all of it.
  - Check your HSA eligibility and 2026 contribution limit (self vs. family).
  - Estimate your predictable medical and dependent-care spending to size FSA elections.
  - Review RSU and bonus holdings at least annually for concentration risk.

The benefit stack most households in their 30s have access to isn't one
decision — it's six, and they don't all carry equal weight. The map orders
them for a reason: employer match first, because turning it down is
declining free money; then the HSA, because of its triple-tax advantage;
then FSAs, sized to spending you already know is coming.

The 2026 limits at the top of the map are the ceiling on each account, not a
target. Most households won't max every bucket in a given year, which is
exactly why the Best-Use Order section matters more than the numbers
themselves — it tells you which dollar to prioritize when you can't fund
everything.

RSUs and bonus planning sit apart from the rest. Unlike a 401(k) or HSA,
there's no contribution limit to hit — the risk is concentration, not
under-funding, and it needs a yearly look rather than a one-time enrollment
decision.
