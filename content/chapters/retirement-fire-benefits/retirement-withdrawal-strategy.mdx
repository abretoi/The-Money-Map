keyTakeaways:
  - A lower withdrawal rate requires a larger portfolio for the same spending level, but produces a bigger margin of safety.
  - The classic 4.0% rule pairs $90k of spending with a $2.25M portfolio need in the ladder shown.
  - Sequence-of-returns risk matters most in the early years of retirement, which is why early retirees often need a more conservative withdrawal rate.
  - >-
      Retirement income is layered: portfolio withdrawals do the early work, while Social Security becomes a later-life income floor that reduces portfolio strain.

actionSteps:
  - Divide your target annual spending by each withdrawal rate on the ladder to see the range of portfolio sizes it implies.
  - Identify where your current portfolio falls on the ladder relative to your spending target.
  - Check whether your plan accounts for sequence-of-returns risk in the first several years of retirement.
  - Review whether your spending plan has built-in flexibility for down markets.

The same spending number produces a very different portfolio target depending
on the withdrawal rate applied to it. At $60,000 of annual spending, the map
shows a portfolio need ranging from $1.20M at a 5.0% withdrawal rate up to
$1.71M at 3.5% — the same income, nearly $500,000 apart in required savings.

The Withdrawal-Rate Ladder makes the same point across different spending
levels, running from a very conservative 3.0% rate up through a higher
sequence-risk 5.0% rate. The 4.0% row is marked as the classic planning rule,
sitting in the middle of the ladder.

The Retirement Income Layers table is a reminder that portfolio withdrawals
are not meant to carry retirement alone forever. Social Security is described
as a later-life income floor that reduces the strain on the portfolio, while
cash and bonds absorb near-term spending and market shocks so the core
portfolio doesn't have to be sold at the wrong time.
