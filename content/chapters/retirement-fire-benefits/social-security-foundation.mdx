keyTakeaways:
  - The 2026 Social Security wage base is $184,500, with employees and employers each paying 6.2% (max employee tax of $11,439).
  - Benefits are calculated from your highest 35 inflation-adjusted earning years, so low-earnings years can drag the formula down.
  - Maximum-benefit examples range from $2,969/month at age 62 to $5,181/month at age 70 — but the average retired worker received about $1,976/month in 2025.
  - Claiming early permanently reduces the benefit; delaying past Full Retirement Age raises it up to age 70.

actionSteps:
  - Check your own Social Security statement for your actual Full Retirement Age and projected benefit at 62, FRA, and 70.
  - Note how many years of your 35-year earnings record are low or zero, and whether continued work could raise your average.
  - Decide whether claiming early, at FRA, or at 70 fits your own longevity expectations and other income sources.
  - Treat Social Security as a foundation layer in your retirement plan rather than your only source of income.

Social Security is often treated as a single number — "my benefit" — when it
is actually the output of a formula with several moving, controllable inputs.
This map lays out those inputs before getting to the number itself.

The top strip covers the mechanics: a $184,500 wage base, a 6.2% employee tax
rate, and the requirement of four credits per year (earned at $7,560 in
2026) to qualify. The claiming-age snapshot is the section most people jump
to — it shows maximum-benefit examples ranging from $2,969/month at 62 to
$5,181/month at 70, alongside the more representative average of about
$1,976/month in 2025.

The "How Social Security Works" table connects the two halves: it's the
35-year formula, plus the decision to claim early or delay, that determines
where an individual actually lands between those maximum and average figures.
The Practical Social Security Rules close the map with a reminder that this
benefit is meant to be a foundation, not the entire retirement plan.
