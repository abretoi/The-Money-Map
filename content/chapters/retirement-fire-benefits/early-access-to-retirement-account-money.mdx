keyTakeaways:
  - Retirement accounts are not uniformly locked until 59½ — the Rule of 55, 72(t)/SEPP payments, Roth contribution basis, and HSA medical withdrawals all offer earlier paths.
  - Early access and tax-free access are two separate questions; a withdrawal can dodge the penalty and still owe income tax.
  - A taxable brokerage account is described as the "best bridge account" precisely because it carries no age restriction.
  - The 20% penalty for non-medical HSA withdrawals before 65 is twice the 10% penalty that applies to most other early retirement account withdrawals.

actionSteps:
  - Identify which of your accounts (401(k), IRA, Roth, HSA, taxable) you might need to draw from before age 59½.
  - If you plan to retire in your mid-50s, check whether your current employer's 401(k) plan qualifies for the Rule of 55.
  - Total up your taxable brokerage balance as a potential pre-59½ bridge fund.
  - Before taking any early distribution, separate the penalty question from the tax question and model both.

Retirement accounts have a reputation for being locked until 59½. That
reputation is only half true. This map lays out five separate doors that open
earlier — the Rule of 55, 72(t)/SEPP payments, original Roth contributions,
HSA medical withdrawals, and the taxable brokerage account that never had a
lock in the first place.

The insight worth sitting with is the one called out right under the core
rules: *early access does not always mean tax-free access*. A 401(k)
distribution under the Rule of 55 dodges the 10% penalty but is still taxed
as ordinary income. Keeping the penalty question and the tax question
separate is what prevents an expensive surprise.

The account-by-account table and the strategy list are built to be read
together — the strategy is the plan, the table is the fine print that
determines whether the plan actually works for a specific account.
