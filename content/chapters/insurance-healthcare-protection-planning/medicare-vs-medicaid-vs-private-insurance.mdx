keyTakeaways:
  - The 2025 Medicare Part B premium is $185 per month with a $257 Part B deductible — Medicare is predictable but rarely $0 across the board.
  - Average employer-plan premiums run $8,951/year for single coverage and $25,572/year for family coverage, with workers paying $1,368 and $6,296 of that respectively.
  - Medicaid is generally the lowest-cost option but eligibility and provider access vary by state.
  - Some people qualify for both Medicare and Medicaid at once ("dual eligible"), which unlocks extra help and lower out-of-pocket costs.

actionSteps:
  - Check whether your household could qualify for Marketplace subsidies based on income and household size.
  - If you're on a high-deductible health plan, confirm you have no disqualifying coverage before contributing to an HSA.
  - Mark Medicare AEP (Oct 15–Dec 7) and Marketplace Open Enrollment on your calendar and review coverage every year.
  - If approaching 65, start comparing Medigap, Part D, and Medicare Advantage options before your enrollment window opens.

Three different systems cover U.S. health care, and they don't compete for
the same people — eligibility, not preference, usually decides which one
applies. Medicare is built around age and disability, Medicaid around income
and state rules, and private insurance around employment or the ACA
Marketplace.

The comparison table makes the trade-offs concrete. Medicare's premium
structure is public and predictable — a Part B premium and deductible set at
the federal level — while private employer coverage varies enormously by
plan, with average total premiums nearly three times higher for family
coverage than single coverage.

The "When Each Type Usually Matters" section is the practical payoff: your
age, income, employment status, and disability status point toward a
specific system, and for some households two systems apply at once through
dual eligibility. None of this is a one-time decision — annual enrollment
windows exist because circumstances and plans both change year to year.
