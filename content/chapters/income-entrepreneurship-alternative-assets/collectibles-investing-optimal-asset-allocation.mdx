keyTakeaways:
  - Collectibles work best as a satellite allocation of about 0%-10% of investable assets, after core savings and retirement accounts are funded.
  - The allocation ladder runs from a 0%-2% hobby sleeve up to a 15%+ risk zone that isn't ideal for most households.
  - Value is driven by authenticity, scarcity, demand, liquidity, and the frictions of storage, insurance, and fees.
  - Long-term collectible gains can be taxed up to 28% federally, which erodes headline returns.

actionSteps:
  - Confirm your emergency fund and retirement accounts are funded before sizing a collectibles sleeve.
  - Decide which rung of the allocation ladder fits your situation and set a percentage ceiling.
  - Track your cost basis, fees, and taxes on any collectible so you know net, not gross, returns.
  - Write down an exit plan for where and how quickly you could sell if you needed the cash.

Collectibles investing gets treated as either a joke or a strategy, and the
map rejects both framings. The Big Idea is explicit: collectibles can
complement wealth building, but diversification still comes first. Core
accounts — emergency fund, retirement — come before any collectibles sleeve.

The allocation ladder is the most useful structure here because it turns a
vague hobby into a bounded decision. A 0%-2% hobby sleeve carries almost no
financial risk; a 15%+ position is flagged directly as a risk zone unsuitable
for most households. Where you sit on that ladder should track your
expertise and liquidity needs, not just enthusiasm.

The value drivers table and the 28% federal tax note are the two pieces
collectors most often skip. Authenticity, scarcity, and liquidity determine
whether an item can actually be sold at a fair price — and taxes, storage,
and fees mean the gross price on a sale is never the number that matters.
