keyTakeaways:
  - The emergency ladder scales from a $7.6k one-month starter cushion up to a $68.4k nine-month high-security fund.
  - Fund size should match income stability — 6 months for variable income or one-income households, 9 months for self-employed or high-risk situations.
  - Emergency cash belongs in high-yield savings, money market funds, or Treasury bills — not the stock market.
  - A fund used during an emergency should be refilled before lifestyle spending increases again.

actionSteps:
  - Calculate your own 1-, 3-, 6-, and 9-month fund targets from your actual monthly spending.
  - Decide which rung of the emergency ladder fits your job stability and household situation.
  - Move any emergency cash currently sitting in a plain checking account into high-yield savings or a money market fund.
  - If you've drawn down your fund for an emergency, set a refill plan before adding new discretionary spending.

An emergency fund isn't one number — it's a ladder, and this map lays out
four rungs against a single reference household spending $91,229/year
($7,602/month). A 1-month cushion covers $7.6k; a 9-month fund covers $68.4k.
Which rung is right depends entirely on how stable your income is.

The "Where to Keep It" table is a reminder that an emergency fund is a
liquidity tool, not a growth tool. High-yield savings, money market funds,
and Treasury bills all trade some yield for safety and access — which is
exactly the tradeoff an emergency fund is supposed to make.

The four practical rules close the loop: cover true emergencies only, don't
chase market returns with this money, size the fund to your income
volatility, and refill it before you let lifestyle spending creep back up.
