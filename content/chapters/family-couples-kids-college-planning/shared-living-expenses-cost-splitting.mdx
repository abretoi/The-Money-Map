keyTakeaways:
  - A shared household budget breaks down into concrete per-person numbers once you list every category, not just rent.
  - 50/50, income-based, and hybrid splits can produce very different per-person totals from the same $3,300 household budget.
  - What gets shared and what stays personal is a decision worth making explicitly, not by default.
  - A joint account, automatic transfers, written rules, and a monthly review reduce friction more than the split method itself.

actionSteps:
  - List every shared expense category your household actually has, not just rent and utilities.
  - Calculate what each split method (50/50, income-based, hybrid) would mean for your own numbers.
  - Agree in writing on which categories are shared and which stay personal.
  - Set up a joint bills account with automatic transfers sized to your chosen split.

Splitting a household budget rarely fails because of the math — it fails
because the categories were never listed and the method was never agreed on.
The table above is a full illustrative build-out: rent, utilities, internet,
groceries, supplies, streaming, insurance, and shared transport, adding to
$3,300 a month between two adults.

The same $3,300 produces different per-person numbers depending on the split
method. A straight 50/50 split charges each adult $1,650 regardless of
income. An income-based split ties the share to what each person earns. A
hybrid approach splits only the essentials by income and leaves personal or
fun spending separate — a middle path between fairness and independence.

None of the three methods is "correct." What matters is agreeing on one,
writing it down, and deciding up front what counts as shared versus personal
— because that line, not the split percentage, is where most disagreements
actually start.
