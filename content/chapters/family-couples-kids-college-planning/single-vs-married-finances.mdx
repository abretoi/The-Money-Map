keyTakeaways:
  - Married-couple family household median income ($128,700) is well above the overall U.S. household median ($83,730) in 2024.
  - Account contribution limits — 401(k), IRA, HSA — apply per person, so married couples can shelter roughly double what a single filer can.
  - >-
      Emergency fund targets differ by structure: 6–12 months of expenses for singles versus 3–6 months for married households, since married households can pool income resilience.
  - Married filing jointly's standard deduction ($30,000) is exactly double the single filer's ($15,000).

actionSteps:
  - Compare your household's income against the relevant median figure for your household type.
  - Calculate your emergency fund target using the 6–12 month (single) or 3–6 month (married) guidance.
  - If married, confirm whether you and your spouse are each maximizing your own 401(k), IRA, and HSA limits.
  - Schedule a recurring joint cash-flow meeting if you are part of a married household.

Household structure changes financial strategy in ways that go beyond
"two incomes are better than one." This map lines up seven money areas —
income structure, housing, insurance, taxes, emergency funds, investing
accounts, and estate planning — side by side for singles and married couples.

The pattern that repeats across nearly every row is resilience versus
concentration. A single household carries 100% of every risk (income loss,
insurance costs, a single set of accounts) while a married household can
often share and diversify those same risks — which is also why the
recommended emergency fund target is larger for singles (6–12 months) than
for married households (3–6 months).

What doesn't change with marriage is the per-person nature of retirement
account limits. A married couple isn't sharing one 401(k) or IRA limit — each
spouse gets their own, which is a meaningful planning lever the "Best
Practices" section builds directly into its recommendation to run a joint
cash-flow meeting and coordinate contributions together.
