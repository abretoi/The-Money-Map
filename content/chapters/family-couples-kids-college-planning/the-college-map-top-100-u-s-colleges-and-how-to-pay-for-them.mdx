keyTakeaways:
  - Sticker price is almost never what a family actually pays — the net price after aid is the real number.
  - Early-career salary by major matters as much as the school; cost and outcome should be read together.
  - A public or in-state option can deliver a similar outcome at a fraction of the annual cost.
  - Keeping total borrowing under roughly 10% of expected salary is the map's debt guardrail.

actionSteps:
  - Run the net price calculator for every school on your list before comparing.
  - Complete the FAFSA — it gates federal aid regardless of income.
  - Look up early-career salaries for your intended major, not just the school.
  - Compare at least one in-state or community-college path against your top choice.

The College Map reframes a college decision as a **cost-versus-outcome** problem,
not a prestige ranking. Every school carries two numbers that matter together:
the total annual cost and the early-career salary its graduates typically earn.

The ranking makes the spread visible. Note how a public flagship can sit near an
Ivy on outcomes while costing less than half as much per year — Penn State and
Berkeley both appear in the top 20 at roughly a third of their private peers'
sticker cost.

The bottom half of the map is the part families most often skip: the funding
options and debt strategy. The single most important habit is running each
school's **net price calculator** before comparing, because the sticker price is
almost never what anyone actually pays.
