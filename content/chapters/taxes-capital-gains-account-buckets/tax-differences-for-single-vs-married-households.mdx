keyTakeaways:
  - Married filing jointly roughly doubles the standard deduction ($15,000 to $30,000) and most bracket widths compared to single filers.
  - The SALT deduction cap of $10,000 applies as a total, not per spouse — a structural driver of the marriage penalty for high-earning couples.
  - Most couples experience a marriage bonus from doubled brackets, but couples with two similarly high incomes can experience a penalty.
  - Capital gains harvested below the 0% threshold ($48,350 single / $96,700 MFJ) are taxed at 0% federally.

actionSteps:
  - Compare your combined taxable income against both the single and MFJ bracket tables to see where the marriage bonus or penalty applies.
  - Check whether your itemized deductions, including the $10,000 SALT cap, exceed your standard deduction.
  - Coordinate with your spouse on dependent claims, retirement contributions, and HSA contributions before filing.
  - Review whether your capital gains fall under the 0% long-term threshold for your filing status.

Filing status changes more than a checkbox on a tax return — it changes which
bracket table applies, how large the standard deduction is, and whether a
household's combined income triggers phaseouts that neither spouse would hit
alone.

The bracket map makes the "doubling" pattern visible directly: married filing
jointly brackets are, for most rates, exactly double the single thresholds.
That's the source of the marriage bonus for couples with uneven incomes. But
a few provisions don't double — the SALT deduction cap sits at $10,000
whether filing single or jointly — which is why two high, similar incomes can
produce a marriage penalty instead.

The real planning levers section is where this becomes actionable. Filing
status, withholding, dependent claims, retirement and HSA contributions, and
capital gains timing are all decisions a couple can coordinate on — and
coordination is the theme the map keeps returning to.
