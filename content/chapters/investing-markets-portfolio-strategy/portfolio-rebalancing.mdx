keyTakeaways:
  - Rebalancing is risk control, not an attempt to time the market.
  - A common drift trigger is about a 5 percentage point move away from target — for example, 80/20 drifting to 88/12.
  - Tax-advantaged accounts (IRA, 401(k)) are usually the easiest place to rebalance without triggering taxable events.
  - Simpler rebalancing plans are followed more consistently than complex ones.

actionSteps:
  - Write down your target asset mix (for example, 80% stocks / 20% bonds).
  - Check your current allocation against that target and calculate the drift in percentage points.
  - If drift exceeds your trigger threshold, direct new contributions toward the underweight asset first.
  - Prefer trading inside tax-advantaged accounts before selling in a taxable brokerage account.

A portfolio that isn't rebalanced doesn't stay put — it drifts toward
whatever asset class is winning. An 80/20 mix left alone through a strong
stock run can quietly become 88/12, carrying more risk than was originally
chosen.

The five-step ladder treats rebalancing as a maintenance loop rather than a
one-time decision: set a target, watch for drift past a trigger, use new
contributions to correct the imbalance where possible, and only sell when
necessary — preferably inside a tax-advantaged account where trades don't
create a tax bill.

The methods table makes clear there's no single right cadence. Calendar-based
rebalancing is simplest to follow; threshold-based rebalancing gives tighter
control; using cash flow (new contributions or dividends) to correct drift is
the most tax-efficient path for a taxable account, since it avoids selling
appreciated positions altogether.
